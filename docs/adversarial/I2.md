# Zycord — Implementation Findings I2

**Scope:** the M1 node — `node/storage`, `node/chain`, `node/mempool`, `node/miner`, `node/rpc`, `cmd/zycordd`, the wallet CLI and its policy layer — plus R3-1, the consensus-parameter binding of the genesis id.

**Persona:** the implementer again, but the question has changed. M0 asked *does the spec survive contact with a compiler?* M1 asks *does the node survive contact with an operating system* — crashes, restarts, disks, clocks. The compiler and the differential fold cannot see this layer, so most of what follows was found by a test that exists only because a guardrail demanded it.

---

## CRITICAL

### I2-C1 — The storage engine is written here, not imported ✅ *deviation from the decisions log*

**The decision being deviated from.** Architecture §21: *"Pebble over LevelDB/Badger — pure Go, actively maintained; keeps cgo count at exactly one (RandomX)."*

**What the decision weighed, and what it did not.** It weighed cgo and maintenance. It did not weigh the dependency tree, because at the time there was no tree to weigh. Adding Pebble pulls **26 modules**, including a Sentry SDK, a Prometheus client, protobuf and a compression library — into a project whose entire case for being trusted is that there is very little of it to read, whose builds must be byte-reproducible, and which is anonymous enough that an inert-but-present telemetry SDK is a genuinely bad shape.

**What the requirement actually is.** One sentence: *a batch is durable or it is absent, never partial.* Everything M1-G1 through G3 asks for follows from that, and none of it is about LSM performance.

**Resolution.** `node/storage` is a write-ahead log plus periodic snapshots, ~830 lines of code across `store.go` and the two platform files, standard library only (it was ~300 when this was written; crash recovery, directory locking and replay hardening are what it grew for, and every line of the growth is recovery reasoning). A batch is one length-prefixed, checksummed record; it is fsynced before it becomes visible in memory; recovery replays intact records and truncates the log at a genuine torn tail — the record header carries a sequence number and its own checksum, separate from the one over the payload, so recovery can establish that a damaged record's frame is genuine before it decides what the damage means, and can therefore tell a torn tail apart from interior corruption with intact records after it, refusing to open rather than repairing the latter by deletion. Compaction writes a snapshot, renames it, then truncates the log, and replay is idempotent so a crash inside that window is harmless. A failed write or fsync on the durability path is terminal for the store rather than silently survivable, and `Open` takes an exclusive lock on the directory so a second process cannot interleave writes into the same log. One transaction may span several records when it is too large for one — a reorg within the declared undo horizon carries up to `UNDO_DEPTH` blocks at `BLOCK_BYTE_CAPACITY` each, gigabytes against a per-record bound of one, and that bound exists to stop a corrupt length field from making the reader allocate arbitrarily, not to make a legal reorg uncommittable. The mechanism is the commit record every journalling design uses: each record carries how many further records belong to the same transaction, replay holds a transaction's parts without applying any of them until it reads the one that declares itself last, and an incomplete transaction is cut away rather than left where a later commit could complete it. Atomicity is therefore per transaction, not per record, and the reorg path (`node/chain` `switchTo`) is its only caller.

**What this costs.** The live view is held in memory, so the engine is bounded by RAM. That matches the spec's own storage target (§14: the hot set in RAM, the fold never blocking on disk) and is sufficient for Era 0, but it is a real ceiling. When state outgrows memory the engine is replaced behind the same interface — and the torture tests written here become the acceptance criteria for whatever replaces it, which is the part worth keeping either way.

**Undo logs and block bodies made that ceiling worse than it needed to be, and only one half is closed.** Both were retained forever with no eviction — undo logs even though `considerBranchLocked` already refuses any reorg deeper than `UNDO_DEPTH`, which makes an undo log beyond that horizon provably unreachable, not merely unlikely to be read again. That half is closed: `Chain.pruneUndoLocked` deletes an undo log the instant it crosses the horizon, staged into the same batch as the commit that pushes the horizon forward, so it inherits this section's atomicity guarantee for free instead of needing a second one. Block bodies are not closed. This engine has no on-disk index that would let a body live outside the hot map — unlike geth's freezer, which does exactly that — so bounding body residency without also losing the ability to serve deep history needs a real cold tier, not a deletion knob, and that is a bigger piece of work than the closed half took on. Until it lands, unbounded body growth — proportional to chain length and block fullness, worked out to be up to several GB/day at the genesis byte limit under sustained full blocks — is an explicit, accepted Era-0 cost, on the same terms as the RAM ceiling above, not a silent one.

**Rejected alternatives.** Pebble, on the dependency argument above — reopen it the moment state size, not correctness, is the binding constraint. LevelDB or Badger: same tree problem, less maintenance. A single-file mmap: fewer moving parts than a log, but crash semantics that are much harder to reason about and impossible to torture-test at every byte offset.

**One dependency does exist**, and it is not this: `golang.org/x/crypto` for Argon2id in wallet key files. It is confined to `wallet/` — `core/` and `node/` are both third-party-free, enforced by `make check-imports` — and Argon2id is not something to hand-roll. That asymmetry is the point: a Go-team crypto primitive in the wallet is a different proposition from a telemetry SDK in the consensus path.

The engine is live and the deviation is no longer recorded only here: [ARCHITECTURE §14](../ARCHITECTURE.md) is the current account of it — the key prefixes, the in-memory hot overlay, what prunes and what never does, and the reversal of the decisions log's Pebble entry — and it carries the two things that came after this finding, the incremental epoch-state root and the map high-water rule.

## HIGH

### I2-H1 — The genesis id did not commit to the parameters ✅ *R3-1, spec gap*

**Problem.** The genesis header carried `GenesisTime`, `GenesisTarget` and the state root — which covers the initial base fees and the beacon — and nothing else. It did not commit to `ChainID`, `TTL_MAX`, the gas schedule, the block ceilings, or any of the other 30-odd parameters. Two nodes configured differently would derive the *same* genesis id, accept each other's blocks, and diverge at the first evaluation of whichever rule they disagreed about. A silent fork whose trigger is a configuration file.

**Fix.** `Params.ConsensusRoot()` digests every consensus parameter by reflective struct walk, and the genesis block carries it in `ParentID` — a field that is otherwise unused at height 0, so the binding costs zero bytes in every later block. "The parameters are what the chain descends from" turns out to be both true and free.

The walk is reflective rather than a hand-written list *because* a forgotten field is the silent-fork vector this exists to close, and the same walk enforces the positivity invariant, so both jobs fail together. A parameter of a type the walk does not recognise panics rather than being silently uncommitted.

**Boundary.** Every `json`-tagged field is consensus unless marked `consensus:"-"`. Exactly one field is excluded — `notes` — and a test pins that list, so moving a field across the boundary is a visible change rather than a struct-tag side effect. The failure mode of forgetting the tag is *over*-committing, which is noisy rather than silent.

**Consequence at the storage boundary.** `chain.Open` refuses a directory whose stored network id does not match this build's genesis id. A node restarted with an edited parameter file stops rather than continuing onto a chain it now disagrees with.

The binding is live, and the wire half of it is owned by [spec/wire.md §4](../spec/wire.md) rather than by this document: `network_id` in the handshake *is* the genesis block id, a receiver whose own differs MUST close the connection before parsing anything further, and — the receiving side of the rule, which stating only the sending side did not cover — no other message kind may precede that check.

### I2-H2 — Genesis was committed through a second code path ✅ *found by writing G2*

**Problem.** The first draft of `chain.Open` folded genesis and wrote its state with bespoke code, separate from the commit path every later block used. Two commit paths, one of which ran exactly once per node lifetime and was therefore the least-tested code in the system while being the foundation of everything above it.

**Fix.** Genesis folds and commits through `commit()` like any other block. Found by `TestGenesisIsCommittedLikeAnyBlock`, which was written to check restart equivalence and instead noticed the duplication.

## MEDIUM

### I2-M1 — The undo log had to become a storage format ✅

Reorgs need the undo log after a restart, so it stops being an in-memory structure and becomes a serialised one. It is written in the same batch as the block it undoes; a rollback deletes it in the same batch that restores the cells. `TestRollbackRestoresStorageAndState` pins that memory and disk move together — the failure it guards against is a restart resurrecting an abandoned branch.

### I2-M2 — A failed commit had to be able to un-mutate memory ✅

`chain.Apply` folds into memory and *then* commits. If the commit fails, memory has moved and disk has not. The fold's undo log is used to put memory back, so a failed apply leaves the node exactly where it was. Without this, a full disk would produce a node whose memory was one block ahead of its storage — the same class of divergence G1 exists to prevent, arriving through a different door.

### I2-M3 — The block builder needed a dry-run entry point ✅

The spec has the miner "dry-run the fold once over the candidate" (§12) to drop the drops. `fold.SealOutcomes` is that entry point: it runs against a copy and returns outcomes without committing, alongside `SealStateRoot`. Both are explicitly *not* part of the state-transition function — the fold judges blocks, it does not help build them.

One pass suffices, and the reason is worth recording: removing a `DROPPED` certificate frees ceiling space but cannot change any other certificate's outcome, because a drop touches no state.

### I2-M4 — The selection policy existed twice ✅ *M1-G5*

`sim/builder.go` held a block selector and `node/miner` was expected to mirror it. Resolved by extraction rather than by discipline: the policy lives in `node/miner` alone and `sim` calls it. The incentive property tests now test the code that actually assembles blocks, which is the only version worth testing.

## LOW / NOTES

- **I2-L1 — Mempool eviction order is sorted, not arbitrary.** Eviction is policy, not consensus, so two nodes may legitimately differ. But a *single* node whose eviction order came from map iteration would be unreproducible against its own logs, and "reproduce it from the seed" is how every other layer here is debugged. Sorted by id. ✅
- **I2-L2 — The pool re-screens deposits on every block.** A deposit that covered at the old tip may not cover at the new one; a pool that skipped this would keep handing the miner certificates that will `DROP`. ✅
- **I2-L3 — The node holds no key material at all.** Not encrypted, not in memory, not accepted at any endpoint. Signing is in `zcd`, in another process. This is a stronger statement than "keys are protected" and it is enforced by there being no code to protect. ✅
- **I2-L4 — The only RPC write is submission, and it grants nothing.** A submitted certificate meets exactly the rules a peer's would. There are no privileged endpoints because there is nothing to privilege: no key can pause, upgrade, censor or mint (P3). Localhost by default, rate-limited by default. ✅
- **I2-L5 — The node's only clock is one injected function.** `Miner.Now` is the single reading of wall time in the tree, which keeps R1-H2's rule easy to hold: the header time is bounded below by the median-past and not bounded above at all. ✅
- **I2-L6 — Key files are always encrypted, and there is no flag to change that.** Argon2id and AES-256-GCM, parameters stored in the file so a user locked out of this binary can recover with any language's standard library. A key file is never silently overwritten — written to a temp file in the same directory, fsynced, then published under its final name, refusing if that name is already taken — which also fixed a durability gap the original `O_EXCL`-only write had: that write could be torn by a crash, and `O_EXCL` then refused the obvious recovery of rerunning to the same path. Publishing uses an exclusive rename (`renameat2(RENAME_NOREPLACE)`, `renamex_np(RENAME_EXCL)`), which publishes and refuses in one syscall, so both properties hold atomically on FAT32 and exFAT — which have no hard links and are exactly where a cold-storage key backup lives. Two weaker tiers exist below it for unmeasured platforms: a hard link, still atomic, and a check-then-rename whose refusal is not. ✅
- **I2-L7 — Wallet moves are sorted canonically.** The protocol imposes no order, so two orderings are two ids with one effect. Harmless for consensus — reordering invalidates the signatures, so only the signer can produce a variant, and each variant carries its own deposit and its own bill — but fatal for a wallet trying to distinguish a retry from a second payment. Sorting gives idempotency, which is a wallet property the protocol cannot and need not provide (R2-M2). ✅

## Confidence

Flagged for the R3 reader. As instructed, the expected new territory was policy rather than consensus, and that is where these are.

- **Mempool policy numbers are reasonable, not forced.** `MaxCertificates: 20_000`, `MaxPerUnderwriter: 64`, `MinTTLLead: 2`. None is derived from anything. They are the parameters most likely to be wrong in a way that only a public testnet reveals, and they are deliberately *node* policy rather than consensus so that being wrong is survivable — but a default that every node inherits is a de facto standard, and I have no evidence for these three.
- **Eviction is by rule, not by price.** Certificates leave the pool when they expire, commit, or stop covering their deposit. There is no fee-ranked eviction under pressure, so a full pool refuses new arrivals rather than displacing worse-paying ones. That is the wrong shape under real congestion and I know it; it is deferred rather than solved because the right design depends on fee-market behaviour nobody has observed yet.
- **The RPC surface is a guess at what a wallet needs.** It grew from what the CLI turned out to require. Nothing there is dangerous — it is read-only apart from submission — but "what a light client needs" is a design question that has not been asked, and answering it later will change these paths.
- **The storage engine is mine.** I2-C1 is the largest deviation in either implementation review, and unlike the M0 findings it was not forced by the spec being unimplementable — it was a judgement call about dependency weight against battle-testing. The torture tests are strong and cover every byte offset of a commit plus real `SIGKILL`, but a hand-rolled durability layer is the thing in this repository I would most want a second reader on.
- **The rate limiter is a fixed window.** It permits a burst of twice the limit across a window boundary. That is a known and standard imprecision, and for a localhost-by-default read API it is not obviously worth a token bucket — but I chose simplicity over correctness there deliberately, and it is a choice rather than an oversight.

Everything else I am confident is forced: it either falls out of the atomicity requirement, out of R3-1, or out of a guardrail that named the property directly.

**Disposition.** Every ✅ is implemented and covered. M1-G1 is pinned by 196 deterministic crash-offset subtests plus a `SIGKILL` suite; G2 by restart equivalence over 40 blocks; G3 by a corruption test that writes a *well-formed* wrong value, because that is the only corruption a checksum cannot catch. G4 through G8 are covered by the RPC, wallet and network-separation tests.
